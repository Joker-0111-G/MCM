### I. An Evacuation Sweep Strategy Based on Our Findings

Our research indicates that the most efficient sweep strategy is dynamic, quantifiable, and often counter-intuitive.

**1. How to Plan Sweeps:**

- **Dynamic Zoning and Parallel Operations:** For multi-floor or complex layouts (Problems 2 and 3), a "Layered Parallel, Zoned Responsibility" strategy is far superior to a single-line sweep. The model (e.g., M-TSP in Problem 2) automatically calculates the optimal zoning plan to ensure balanced workloads among teams.
- **Environment-Adaptive Search:** The sweep strategy must change as the environment degrades. Our model (Problem 1) quantifies this process: in the initial phase (high visibility), use a "Perimeter Search" for speed; when smoke reduces visibility below a threshold (e.g., 2.5 meters), teams **must** switch to a "Zigzag/Grid-search" to ensure full coverage.

**2. How to Assign Responders:**

- **The "Marginal Utility" of Optimal Team Size:** Adding more responders does not always increase efficiency. Our sensitivity analysis shows a "sweet spot" for team size (e.g., 6 to 10 responders). Beyond a saturation point (e.g., 11+ responders), a "congestion effect" may slightly decrease efficiency. Our model can calculate the optimal cost-benefit ratio for team size in any given layout.
- **Task-Based Specialization:** In complex scenarios (Problem 3), responders should be assigned tasks based on their expertise (e.g., Fire/Security vs. Medical). The model supports prioritizing a medical responder for a pregnant occupant (Apt 1) while fire responders handle an infant (Apt 4) and a complex warehouse (WH Maze).

**3. Strategies to Ensure Redundancy and Speed without Sacrificing Safety:**

- **Speed (Countering the Non-Linear Time Penalty):** A key finding from our sensitivity analysis is that the cost of response delay is **non-linear**. A 600-second delay is not just 600 seconds lost; it also results in an *additional* 887.2 seconds of "environmental cost" as smoke degradation slows subsequent operations. This provides robust data supporting the "golden rescue time" and the absolute necessity of speed.
- **Redundancy (Ensuring Safety):** Our model (Problem 2) shows that increasing from 3 to 4 responders, while offering minimal time savings, provides critical `redundancy`. This extra responder is essential for re-checking areas to guarantee no one is left behind, especially in low-visibility, perfectly aligning with the "without sacrificing safety" principle.

### II. Actionable Recommendations for Strategies

Our model's adaptability (Advantage 7.1) and human-centric focus (Advantage 7.3) allow for specific recommendations.

**1. Based on Different Building Types and Floor Plan Layouts:**

- **Symmetrical Layouts (e.g., Problem 1 Office):** Employ a "symmetric coordination" strategy. Two responders mirroring each other's paths on opposite sides of the hallway is most efficient.
- **Multi-Floor Buildings (e.g., Problem 2 Apartments):** Use the "Layered Parallel" strategy. Our 3D topological model (Problem 2) automatically calculates which `stairwell` (left or right) a responder should use to reach their assigned floor via the shortest path.
- **Heterogeneous Rooms (e.g., Problem 2, Scenario 2):** Our model reveals that **small-volume rooms must be prioritized**. Smoke fills them faster (e.g., 6 min vs. 10 min for large rooms), causing their hazard level to escalate more quickly.
- **Complex/Maze Layouts (e.g., Problem 3 Warehouse):** Standard intuition fails. These require advanced pathfinding (our Dijkstra macro-path + A* micro-path algorithm) to find the true optimal path, which is often obscured by obstacles.

**2. Based on Various Types of Occupants:**

- **High-Risk Occupants (e.g., Infants, Pregnant Women):** These are the highest priority (Level 1) in our model (Problem 3). Responders must be assigned to these areas (Apt 1, Apt 4) first.
- **Standard Occupants (e.g., Shops, Offices):** These areas are swept via the optimal path after high-risk targets are addressed.
